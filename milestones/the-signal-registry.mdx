# ⭐ The Signal Registry

**Milestone completed August 19, 2026.** [Floor 5 on the board](/master-plan/floor-5)

---

## What this is

Everything Follow Up Boss tells us about a client arrives by one of five roads. Some come straight
over the wire the instant they happen. Some are read off the page by a browser we drive on a machine
in the office. One is pieced together from the wording of an automatic note. One arrives as email
directly from Google. And one road is retired and carries nothing at all anymore.

For a long time that list lived in people's heads. If you wanted to know how a particular signal
reached us, you asked whoever had worked on it last - and depending on who you asked, you might get
a different answer, or a confidently wrong one.

The five roads are now written down as a record, one row per road, saying what each road is, what
runs it, where it lands, and which watchman is posted on it. And every one of the **42** rules that
decide what a signal becomes was pointed at the road it *actually* uses, rather than the road
somebody assumed it used.

That distinction turned out to matter. Four rules all arrive inside the same container from Follow Up
Boss, so a naive reading would have filed them all on the same road. Two of them are Zillow in-app
messages, whose actual words only exist after the office machine has swept the page - a completely
different road. Guessing from the container alone would have quietly filed our highest-intent traffic
under the wrong road, and nothing would have looked broken.

## The walkthrough

### You open the arrival map

One findable page, showing all **28** signals grouped by the road they arrive on. For each one: how it
reaches us, which record it lands in, and what it turns into on a person's page.

Before this existed, that question had no single answer anywhere - only fragments across code, chat
history, and memory.

### The map is generated, and that is the whole point

This is the part that stops the map rotting. It is not written by hand and kept up to date by
somebody remembering. It is **produced from the live system**, so what it says is what the system is
actually doing.

A hand-maintained map is correct on the day it is written and drifts away from the truth every day
after. A generated one cannot drift - if a road changes, the map changes with it. That difference is
worth more than the map itself.

### The roads carry their own honesty

Where something is not being watched, the record says so rather than leaving a comfortable blank. The
retired road names no watchman, because it has none, and claiming one would be a lie. The
note-reading road records plainly that a note's *arrival* is watched but its *reading* is not. One
road carries a written reminder that neither of its two health signals is trustworthy on its own -
recorded on the day both of them read healthy while a real client's message sat unrecovered for about
six hours.

### The limits moved with it

Each road carries the numbers that decide when it counts as too quiet. Those used to be values buried
in code, which meant tuning one was a developer's job and a deploy. They are now data on the road's
own row. The proof of that ran both directions: the same stale road judged healthy under one limit
and unhealthy under another, with nothing changed but the number.

## What you can do with it

| What you do | What happens |
|---|---|
| Open the arrival map | All twenty-eight signals, grouped by the road they travel |
| Look up one signal | You see how it reaches us, where it lands, and what it becomes |
| Ask which road a rule uses | The answer is a value on the record, not somebody's recollection |
| Ask who is watching a road | The road names its watchman - or says honestly that it has none |
| Change how quiet is too quiet | Edit the number on the road's row. No developer, no deploy |
| Retire a road | The row says retired, and every page that reads it says so too |
